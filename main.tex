\section{Description}
%=============================================================================

Visual surround suppression is a ubiquitous phenomenon in the primary visual cortex (V1):
the firing rate of a neuron responding optimally to a small grating stimulus is paradoxically
\emph{reduced} when the same stimulus is enlarged to cover its classical surround. This
non-intuitive property is a hallmark of \textbf{Inhibition-Stabilised Networks (ISN)}, a regime
where recurrent inhibition is essential for stability and where a seemingly excitatory stimulus
can ultimately decrease local firing rates.

In this mini-project we build such a network from the ground up using \textbf{Leaky
Integrate-and-Fire (LIF)} neurons. Starting from a population of $N=100$ uncoupled neurons
(Exercise 0), we construct a sparsely connected \textbf{Excitatory-Inhibitory (E-I) network}
operating in the balanced state (Exercise 1). We then assemble $N_\text{units} = 10$ such
E-I units on a one-dimensional ring to form a \textbf{continuous attractor field model} of the
cortical sheet (Exercise 2). Finally, we probe the network with stimuli of varying spatial
extent to reproduce surround suppression and identify its circuit mechanism (Exercise 3).

All simulations are implemented in Python using \texttt{NumPy} and sparse matrices
(\texttt{scipy.sparse}). The differential equation of the LIF neuron is integrated with
the \textbf{Forward Euler} method using a time step $\Delta t = 0.5$ ms.

\section{Exercise 0 --- Population of Unconnected LIF Neurons}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Model and equations}

The membrane potential $u_i(t)$ of neuron $i$ obeys
\begin{equation}
    \tau_m \frac{du_i}{dt} = -u_i(t) + R\,I_i(t),
    \label{eq:lif}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_m = 20$ ms and $R = 1$ M$\Omega$. When $u_i$ reaches the threshold $\theta = 20$ mV,
a spike is fired and the potential is instantaneously reset to $u_\text{reset} = -10$ mV.
The stochastic background input is modelled as
\begin{equation}
    I_i^\text{bg}(t_k) \sim 1\,\text{nA} \cdot \text{Poisson}\!\left(\bar{n}_\text{bg}\right),
    \quad \bar{n}_\text{bg} = 25,
    \label{eq:bg}
\end{equation}
sampled independently for each neuron and each time step.

\subsection{Ex 0.1 --- Rasterplot under oscillatory input}

A population of $N = 100$ unconnected neurons receives the synchronous oscillatory input
$I_i^\text{ext}(t) = I_0\bigl(1 + \sin(\omega t)\bigr)$ with $I_0 = 20$ nA and
$\omega = 10$ rad/s, for $T = 1000$ ms. Neurons are initialised uniformly in
$[u_\text{reset},\,\theta]$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images/fig_0_1_raster.png}
    \caption{%
        \textbf{Top:} Rasterplot of 100 unconnected LIF neurons under a shared sinusoidal
        drive (no background noise). \textbf{Bottom:} Mean population firing rate (Hz, blue)
        overlaid with the scaled input current (orange dashed). The network fires in
        synchronised bursts that track the positive half-cycles of $I(t)$.%
    }
    \label{fig:ex01}
\end{figure}

Because all neurons receive an identical deterministic input and start from the same uniform
distribution of potentials, the entire population crosses threshold almost simultaneously
at each positive half-cycle, producing the synchronous bursts visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:ex01}.
There is no noise to break this symmetry.

\subsection{Ex 0.2 --- $f$--$I$ curve with background noise}

The theoretical $f$--$I$ curve for the LIF neuron (without noise) is:
\begin{equation}
    r(I_0) = \left[\tau_m\,\ln\!\frac{R I_0 - u_\text{reset}}{R I_0 - \theta}\right]^{-1},
    \quad \text{valid for } RI_0 > \theta.
    \label{eq:fi_theory}
\end{equation}

We simulate $N = 100$ neurons with the Poisson background current for each $I_0 \in [-10,\,50]$
nA (steps of 5 nA), simulating $T = 100$ ms and averaging the firing rate over the last 50 ms.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/fig_0_2_fi.png}
    \caption{%
        $f$--$I$ curve of the LIF neuron. Circles: simulation with Poisson background noise.
        Crosses: theoretical curve Eq.~\eqref{eq:fi_theory} (no noise). The stochastic input
        rounds the sharp threshold and shifts the onset of firing to lower currents.%
    }
    \label{fig:ex02}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Key differences (Fig.~\ref{fig:ex02}):} The theoretical curve has a step-like onset
exactly at $I_\text{thr} = \theta/R = 20$ nA. With Poisson background noise, fluctuations can push
$u_i$ above $\theta$ even when $I_0 < 20$ nA, generating \emph{noise-driven} spikes below the
deterministic threshold. This smooths and left-shifts the $f$--$I$ relationship, consistent
with the well-known effect of additive noise on threshold crossing.

\section{Exercise 1 --- Excitation-Inhibition Balance}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Network definition}

We add recurrent synaptic interactions: the total current to neuron $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    I_i(t) = \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{ij}\,s_j(t - \tau_\text{delay})}_{\displaystyle I_i^\text{syn}(t)}
    + I_i^\text{ext}(t) + I_i^\text{bg}(t),
    \label{eq:total_current}
\end{equation}
where $s_j(t)$ is the spike train of neuron $j$. The synaptic weights are
\begin{equation}
    w_{E\leftarrow E} = w_{I\leftarrow E} = J, \qquad
    w_{E\leftarrow I} = w_{I\leftarrow I} = -gJ.
    \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
The network is sparse: each neuron receives exactly $K_E = pN_E$ excitatory and
$K_I = pN_I$ inhibitory connections, chosen uniformly at random. The weight matrix
is stored as a \texttt{scipy.sparse.csr\_matrix} for memory efficiency. Network parameters:
$N_E = 1000$, $\gamma = N_I/N_E = 0.25$, $p = 0.02$, $g = 5$, $J = 45$ pC,
$\tau_\text{delay} = 2$ ms.

\subsection{Ex 1.2 --- Balanced state and $f$--$I$ curves}

The network is said to be in the \textbf{balanced state} when the excitatory and inhibitory
populations fire at approximately the same mean rate across all input levels.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/fig_1_2_fi_net.png}
    \caption{%
        $f$--$I$ curves for the E and I populations of the sparse recurrent network.
        The two curves nearly overlap across the entire range of $I_0$, confirming
        operation in the balanced state ($r_E \approx r_I$).%
    }
    \label{fig:ex12}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Ex 1.3 --- Expected synaptic input}

\textbf{Derivation.} We split the synaptic sum in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total_current} into
excitatory and inhibitory parts. Neuron $i$ receives $K_E$ excitatory contacts each of
weight $J$ and $K_I$ inhibitory contacts each of weight $-gJ$:
\begin{align}
    \bigl\langle I_i^\text{syn}(t) \bigr\rangle
    &= J \sum_{j\in E} \langle s_j(t-\tau_d)\rangle - gJ \sum_{j\in I} \langle s_j(t-\tau_d)\rangle.
\end{align}
In the limit of many neurons with fixed $K_E, K_I$, the average spike train of any neuron
of type $A$ satisfies $\langle s_j^A \rangle = r_A(t)$. Using $K_I = pN_I = p\gamma N_E = \gamma K_E$:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        \bigl\langle I_i^\text{syn}(t) \bigr\rangle = J K_E\bigl(r_E(t) - g\gamma\,r_I(t)\bigr).
    }
    \label{eq:isyn}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Ex 1.4 --- Inhibition-dominated regime}

The network is \textbf{inhibition-dominated} when the inhibitory contribution exceeds the excitatory
one in Eq.~\eqref{eq:isyn}. Since $r_E \approx r_I$ in the balanced state, this requires
$g\gamma > 1$, i.e. $g > 1/\gamma = 4$.

With the chosen parameters ($g = 5 > 4$), the condition is satisfied. To illustrate the
consequence of violating it, we simulated the network with $g = 3.0 < 4$ and $I_i^\text{ext} = 0$:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/fig_1_4_runaway.png}
    \caption{%
        Runaway activity when $g = 3.0$ (inhibition dominance condition violated).
        Both E and I firing rates grow without bound: excitation overwhelms inhibition,
        causing catastrophic positive feedback. With $g = 5$, both populations remain
        at low stable rates.%
    }
    \label{fig:ex14}
\end{figure}

When $g < 4$, the inhibitory feedback is too weak to cancel the recurrent excitation.
The net synaptic current in Eq.~\eqref{eq:isyn} becomes positive and grows with $r_E$,
driving a runaway instability.

\subsection{Ex 1.5 \& 1.6 --- Selective population stimulation}

We apply a time-varying input ($I_0 = 10$ nA, $\omega = 25$ rad/s) selectively to
the E population during $0\text{--}500$ ms and then to the I population during $500\text{--}1000$ ms
(Eqs.~6--7 of the problem sheet).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/fig_1_5_stim.png}
    \caption{%
        \textbf{Top:} Rasterplot of 100 E neurons (blue) and 25 I neurons (red). The vertical
        dashed line marks the switch from E to I stimulation at $t = 500$ ms.
        \textbf{Bottom:} Population firing rates in 20 ms bins.
        During E-only stimulation (left half), both populations increase their rate, but E
        leads. During I-only stimulation (right half), E is suppressed and I also drops
        — the \emph{paradoxical} effect.%
    }
    \label{fig:ex15}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Discussion (Ex 1.6):}

\textit{(i) Why does stimulating only one population break the balance?}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:isyn}, balanced state operation requires $r_E \approx r_I$, so
$\langle I^\text{syn}\rangle \approx 0$. When extra current is injected exclusively into
E neurons, $r_E$ rises while $r_I$ initially lags. The asymmetry creates a non-zero net
synaptic drive that disrupts the tightly coupled $r_E = r_I$ fixed point, causing
transient rate differences until a new (unbalanced) equilibrium is reached.

\textit{(ii) Paradoxical inhibitory response.}
This network operates in the \textbf{ISN regime}: recurrent inhibition provides stability,
and the E population sustains I activity via $w_{I\leftarrow E} = J$. When additional current
is injected into I neurons, their firing rate initially rises, which increases the inhibitory
feedback onto E neurons. The suppressed E population then drives \emph{less} excitatory
input back onto I, reducing I's recurrent excitation. Because I neurons depend on E for
their tonic drive, this secondary effect outweighs the injected current, ultimately
\emph{decreasing} I's firing rate relative to the direct-stimulation expectation
--- a signature uniquely diagnostic of the ISN regime \cite{ozeki2009}.

\section{Exercise 2 --- Field Model of the Cortical Sheet}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Architecture}

We chain $N_\text{units} = 10$ E-I units on a periodic ring. Positions
$x_\alpha \in [0,1)$ are equally spaced. Unit-to-unit interactions are defined by
\begin{align}
    W^{E\leftarrow E}_{\alpha\beta} &= W_0\,f(x_\alpha, x_\beta),
    \label{eq:W_EE}\\
    W^{I\leftarrow E}_{\alpha\beta} &= g\gamma W_0\,\bigl(1 - f(x_\alpha, x_\beta)\bigr),
    \label{eq:W_IE}\\
    W^{E\leftarrow I}_{\alpha\beta} &= W^{I\leftarrow I}_{\alpha\beta} = 0,
    \label{eq:W_zero}
\end{align}
where $f(x_\alpha,x_\beta) = \mathbf{1}\bigl[d(x_\alpha,x_\beta)\le\sigma\bigr]$ and
$\sigma = 0.2$ is the excitatory range, with self-connections excluded ($\alpha\neq\beta$).
The choice of zero $I\to E$ and $I\to I$ cross-unit weights reflects the biological finding
that cortical interneurones make only short-range connections; long-range inhibition is
implemented \emph{indirectly} via long-range $E\to I$ projections (Eqs.~\ref{eq:W_IE}).

\subsection{Ex 2.1 --- Field equations}

The total external input received by E-I unit $\alpha$ from all other units is
\begin{align}
    I^{\text{ext},E}_\alpha(t) &= \sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}
    W^{E\leftarrow E}_{\alpha\beta}\,r_E^\beta\!\left(t - \tau_\text{delay}\right),
    \label{eq:IE_field}\\
    I^{\text{ext},I}_\alpha(t) &= \sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}
    W^{I\leftarrow E}_{\alpha\beta}\,r_E^\beta\!\left(t - \tau_\text{delay}\right).
    \label{eq:II_field}
\end{align}
Note that only the excitatory population rates $r_E^\beta$ appear on the right-hand side,
consistent with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:W_EE}--\eqref{eq:W_zero}.

\subsection{Ex 2.2 --- Verification of the balanced state}

With $W_0 = 0$, unit-unit interactions are disabled and each E-I unit reverts to the
isolated balanced network of Exercise 1. We verified that the $f$--$I$ curves of
E and I populations remain matched in each unit, recovering $r_E \approx r_I$
across input levels (result not shown; identical to Fig.~\ref{fig:ex12}).

\subsection{Ex 2.4 --- Spontaneous bump formation ($W_0 = 90$ pC)}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/fig_2_4_bump.png}
    \caption{%
        Excitatory population activity of 10 ring units over $T = 200$ ms with $W_0 = 90$ pC.
        A spatially localised bump of activity emerges spontaneously from background noise,
        typically within the first 50--100 ms.%
    }
    \label{fig:ex24}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Why is the bump stable?}
From Eq.~\eqref{eq:W_EE}, units within radius $\sigma$ of the bump centre excite each other
($W^{E\leftarrow E} = W_0$), sustaining local activity through positive feedback.
Simultaneously, from Eq.~\eqref{eq:W_IE}, those same central excitatory units project
strongly outward ($W^{I\leftarrow E} = g\gamma W_0$), driving the inhibitory populations of
\emph{distant} units and suppressing their activity. This ``Mexican hat'' lateral interaction
--- short-range excitation, long-range inhibition --- creates a stable fixed point around
any position on the ring, defining a \textbf{continuous attractor}.

\textbf{Emergence time and location:} The bump typically forms within 50--100 ms.
The specific unit at which it forms varies between runs, as it is selected by the initial
Poisson noise rather than any deterministic property of the network. This is the hallmark
of a continuous attractor: a family of equivalent stable states related by translation.

\subsection{Ex 2.5 --- Minimum $W_0$ for bump formation}

Sweeping $W_0 \in \{50, 60, 70, 80, 90\}$ pC, we observe that a persistent, spatially
localised bump only forms robustly for $W_0 \gtrsim 80$ pC. Below this value the
inter-unit excitation is insufficient to counteract background noise, and activity
remains spread across all units. Thus \textbf{$W_0^\text{crit} \approx 80$ pC}.

\section{Exercise 3 --- Paradoxical Response Properties and Surround Suppression}
%=============================================================================

\subsection{Ex 3.1 --- Step stimulus at unit 5 ($W_0 = 45$ pC)}

We set $W_0 = 45\,\text{pC} < W_0^\text{crit}$ (no spontaneous bump) and apply a step
input $I_0 = 30$ nA to the E population of unit~5 from $t = 100$ ms:
\begin{equation}
    I^{\text{ext},E}_i(t) = \begin{cases} I_0 & t \geq 100\,\text{ms} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
    \quad \text{for neurons } i \in \text{unit 5}.
    \label{eq:step}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.54\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/fig_3_1_step.png}
        \caption{Activity heatmap. Unit 5 is strongly activated while all others are suppressed.}
        \label{fig:ex31}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[b]{0.43\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/fig_3_2_tuning.png}
        \caption{Mean E rate (post-stimulus) vs.\ unit location: the spatial tuning curve.}
        \label{fig:ex32}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{%
        Step stimulation of unit 5. \textbf{(a)} The stimulated unit shows a sharp rate
        increase while surrounding units are suppressed via long-range $W^{I\leftarrow E}$
        projections. \textbf{(b)} The spatial profile acts as a tuning curve (see Ex 3.2).%
    }
    \label{fig:ex31_32}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Observations:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unit~5 increases its excitatory rate sharply after $t = 100$ ms.
    \item All \emph{other} units are \textbf{suppressed} relative to their spontaneous level.
        The mechanism: unit~5's increased $r_E$ drives the inhibitory populations of distant
        units via $W^{I\leftarrow E}_{\alpha 5}$, which then suppress the local E population.
    \item $W_0 < 80$ pC is essential: if a spontaneous bump were already present, the step
        input would merely shift it rather than demonstrating clean stimulus selectivity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ex 3.2 --- Tuning curve interpretation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ex32} shows the mean E rate of each unit (averaged post-stimulus onset) versus
unit location. By the \textbf{translational symmetry} of the ring, the response of unit $\alpha$
to a stimulus at unit~5 is identical to the response of unit~5 when the stimulus is placed
$d(x_\alpha, x_5)$ away. Hence, reinterpreting the horizontal axis as ``distance of stimulus
from the unit of interest'' transforms this spatial profile into the
\textbf{tuning curve} of unit~5 as a function of stimulus location.

\subsection{Ex 3.3 --- Surround suppression curve}

We apply Gaussian stimuli of varying width $\sigma_\text{stim} \in [0,\,0.4]$ (step 0.04)
centred at the location of unit~5:
\begin{equation}
    I^{\text{ext},E}_\alpha(t) = I_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{d(x_\alpha,\,x_0)^2}{2\sigma_\text{stim}^2}\right),
    \quad I_0 = 30\,\text{nA},
    \label{eq:gaussian_stim}
\end{equation}
and record the mean E rate of unit~5 over the last 150 ms of each 200 ms trial.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{images/fig_3_3_suppression.png}
    \caption{%
        \textbf{Surround suppression curve.} Mean excitatory firing rate of unit~5 as a
        function of $\sigma_\text{stim}$. The rate rises initially then falls for
        $\sigma_\text{stim} \gtrsim 0.2$, recapitulating the classical surround suppression
        effect observed in area V1 \cite{ozeki2009}.%
    }
    \label{fig:ex33}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Ex 3.4 --- Mechanism of rate decrease}

For small $\sigma_\text{stim}$, only units within the excitatory range $\sigma = 0.2$ are
substantially driven. Local $W^{E\leftarrow E}$ connections amplify unit~5's response.
Once $\sigma_\text{stim}$ exceeds $\sigma$, peripheral units receive significant input.
These peripheral E populations project strongly onto the \emph{inhibitory} populations of
unit~5 via $W^{I\leftarrow E}$ connections (Eq.~\ref{eq:W_IE}). The resulting surge of
inhibition onto unit~5's E population overcomes the additional direct excitation, causing
the observed rate decrease.

The inflection point in Fig.~\ref{fig:ex33} occurs near $\sigma_\text{stim} \approx \sigma = 0.2$,
directly reflecting the excitatory connectivity range, as predicted by the step function $f$
in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:W_EE}--\eqref{eq:W_IE}.

\subsection{Ex 3.5 --- Direct circuit cause and ISN implications}

\textbf{Direct cause: increased inhibition.}
Monitoring the I population of unit~5 separately confirms that its firing rate \emph{increases}
with $\sigma_\text{stim}$, even as the E population is suppressed. It is this increased inhibitory
drive that directly reduces E activity, not reduced excitation.

\textbf{Implication for an experimentalist.}
If one could only measure increased inhibitory activity in the target circuit, the natural
conclusion would be that the network operates in the \textbf{inhibition-dominated} regime
discussed in Exercise~1 --- specifically that it is an ISN. In an ISN, the net recurrent
inhibitory weight exceeds the excitatory one ($g\gamma > 1$, see Ex.~1.4), and extra
excitatory drive to peripheral units paradoxically \emph{increases} local inhibition rather
than local excitation. This is precisely the ISN signature. An experimentalist observing
increased inhibition without knowledge of the circuit would correctly infer an ISN
configuration, consistent with the findings of Ozeki et al.\ (2009) \cite{ozeki2009}.

\section{Exercise 4 --- Final Question (Biology-Oriented)}
%=============================================================================

This mini-project in computational neuroscience should link to neurobiology. We design an
experiment that would test specific predictions of the model.

\textbf{Prediction to test.} The model predicts that surround suppression in this network is
\emph{entirely mediated by increased local inhibition} (Ex.~3.5), which is a signature of the ISN
regime. A critical further prediction is: \textbf{silencing the local inhibitory population should
abolish surround suppression} and convert the response function from bell-shaped back to
monotonically increasing.

\textbf{Experimental design.}
We propose an in-vivo two-photon calcium imaging experiment in head-fixed mice viewing
drifting sinusoidal gratings projected onto a monitor:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Animal preparation.} Use a mouse expressing GCaMP8f in excitatory neurons
    (Emx1-Cre $\times$ Ai148) and the red-shifted calcium indicator jRCaMP1b in
    parvalbumin-positive (PV) interneurones (PV-Cre $\times$ jRCaMP1b), enabling simultaneous
    dual-channel imaging of E and I cells in layer 2/3 of area V1.

    \item \textit{Stimulus protocol.} Present drifting gratings at the optimal orientation and
    spatial frequency of a target E cell. Systematically vary the \emph{outer radius} of an
    annular grating from $0.5°$ to $30°$ in steps of $2°$, while keeping the inner grating disk
    fixed ($2°$ diameter). This isolates surround from centre drive, analogous to increasing
    $\sigma_\text{stim}$ in our model.

    \item \textit{Optogenetic manipulation.} Co-inject an AAV encoding soma-targeted
    halorhodopsin (eNpHR3.0-EYFP) into PV-Cre mice within a confined patch ($\approx 200\,\mu$m
    radius, matching the short-range inhibitory scale). On interleaved trials, illuminate with
    a 593 nm LED to silence PV interneurones specifically during the \emph{surround epoch} only.

    \item \textit{Prediction from the model.} In \emph{control} trials, E cell responses should
    show the characterisic inverted-U surround suppression curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:ex33}).
    In \emph{silencing} trials, removing local inhibition should restore the monotonically
    increasing response, because the circuit mechanism (increased $W^{I\leftarrow E}$ drive) is
    eliminated. A failure to rescue excitatory responses would imply a feedforward (non-ISN)
    mechanism of suppression.

    \item \textit{Additional module.} To make quantitative comparison with the model tractable,
    we would add a calcium indicator dynamics module to the simulation: convolving the simulated
    spike trains with a single-exponential kernel ($\tau_\text{GCaMP} \approx 400$ ms) to predict
    $\Delta F/F$ traces directly comparable to the imaged fluorescence signal.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
%=============================================================================

We have successfully implemented a hierarchical computational model --- from individual LIF
neurons, through a balanced E-I network, to a multi-unit cortical ring attractor --- that
reproduces visual surround suppression from first principles. The key results are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Poisson background noise smooths and left-shifts the $f$--$I$ curve, enabling
    sub-threshold firing absent in the deterministic model.
    \item The sparse recurrent E-I network maintains a \emph{balanced state}
    ($r_E \approx r_I$) and becomes inhibition-dominated for $g > 4$; below this threshold
    the network is unstable.
    \item Asymmetric stimulation of a single population reveals the \emph{paradoxical}
    ISN response: driving inhibitory cells can reduce their own firing rate.
    \item Short-range excitation and long-range inhibition in the ring model support
    spontaneous continuous attractor dynamics (bump formation) for $W_0 \gtrsim 80$ pC.
    \item Surround suppression emerges naturally: firing rate peaks for small stimuli and
    decreases for $\sigma_\text{stim} > \sigma$, driven by \emph{increased inhibition}
    from peripheral units --- a hallmark prediction of ISN theory, consistent with the
    experimental findings of Ozeki et al.\ (2009).
\end{itemize}
